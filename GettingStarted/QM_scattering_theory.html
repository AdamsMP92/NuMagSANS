Quantum Scattering Theory#

\[\begin{split}\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{stationary Schrödinger equation} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{Lippmann--Schwinger equation} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{far-field asymptotics} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{scattering amplitude} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{first Born approximation} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{Fourier transform of the interaction potential} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{nuclear and magnetic scattering operator} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{spin projection and SANS cross sections} \end{array}}\end{split}\]

This page derives the elastic nuclear and magnetic SANS cross sections used by NuMagSANS from a single quantum-mechanical scattering problem. The purpose is not to derive the strong neutron–nucleus interaction from first principles. Instead, the coherent nuclear scattering-length density and the sample magnetization are taken as the effective material fields, and the route from these fields to the spin-resolved cross sections is made explicit.

Fermi’s golden rule provides an equivalent first-order route from the interaction matrix element to the transition rate. It is therefore an alternative to the scattering-state argument at the cross-section step, not an additional approximation that must be inserted into the chain above.

Scope and assumptions#

The derivation describes the model implemented by NuMagSANS under the following assumptions:

  • the sample is static and the scattering is elastic, so that \(|\mathbf{k}_{f}|=|\mathbf{k}_{i}|=k\);

  • nuclear scattering is coherent and spin independent;

  • the neutron is nonrelativistic and the first Born approximation is valid;

  • magnetic scattering is produced by a magnetostatic magnetization field;

  • the nuclear and magnetic amplitudes are added coherently before their absolute square is taken;

  • the incident and analyzed spin states are ideal eigenstates of one chosen polarization axis.

NuMagSANS does not include nuclear spin-incoherent scattering, inelastic sample dynamics, multiple scattering, refraction at interfaces, instrumental resolution, finite polarization efficiency, or spin leakage. These effects require extensions of the theory or experimental corrections.

The small-angle approximation \(q_x\simeq0\) is introduced only when the general result is specialized to the NuMagSANS detector geometry. It is not needed for the scattering-theory steps themselves.

Notation used below#

The same symbols occur in ordinary three-dimensional space and in the two-dimensional neutron spin space. The following distinctions are useful:

  • bold symbols such as \(\mathbf r\), \(\mathbf B\), and \(\mathbf M\) are vectors in real space;

  • a hat on a bold vector, for example \(\widehat{\mathbf q}=\mathbf q/|\mathbf q|\), denotes a unit vector;

  • a hat on a non-bold symbol, for example \(\widehat U\), denotes an operator acting on the two spin components;

  • \(\mathbb I_2\) and \(\mathbb I_3\) are the identity matrices in two-dimensional spin space and three-dimensional real space, respectively;

  • a tilde denotes a spatial Fourier transform, an asterisk denotes complex conjugation, and a dagger denotes a complex-conjugate transpose;

  • the subscripts \(i\) and \(f\) mean initial and final;

  • \(d\sigma/d\Omega\) is the differential cross section of one scattering system, whereas \(d\Sigma/d\Omega=(1/V)d\sigma/d\Omega\) is the macroscopic cross section per sample volume and has units of inverse length.

All real-space nuclear and magnetization fields considered here are real. Their Fourier transforms can nevertheless be complex because spatial phase information is encoded in the Fourier amplitudes.

1. Stationary Schrödinger equation and effective neutron Hamiltonian#

Consider a neutron of mass \(m_n\) in a static sample. Its stationary wavefunction is a two-component spinor. In any chosen orthonormal spin basis \((\chi_1,\chi_2)\), it can be written explicitly as

\[\begin{split}\Psi(\mathbf r) =\psi_1(\mathbf r)\chi_1+\psi_2(\mathbf r)\chi_2 =\begin{pmatrix} \psi_1(\mathbf r)\\ \psi_2(\mathbf r) \end{pmatrix} \in\mathbb C^2.\end{split}\]

The complex functions \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are probability amplitudes for the two basis states. Their local probability density is \(\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2\). The effective low-energy Hamiltonian used throughout this derivation is

\[\boxed{ \widehat H =-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_n}\nabla^2\mathbb I_2 +U_{\mathrm N}(\mathbf r)\mathbb I_2 -\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n\cdot\mathbf B(\mathbf r).}\]

The corresponding stationary Schrödinger equation is

\[\widehat H\Psi(\mathbf r)=E\Psi(\mathbf r).\]

The kinetic-energy operator acts in the same way on both components. The interaction \(\widehat U(\mathbf r)\), in contrast, is a local \(2\times2\) matrix and may mix them. At the level required here it contains a scalar nuclear part and a magnetic Zeeman part,

\[\widehat U(\mathbf r) =U_{\mathrm N}(\mathbf r)\mathbb I_2 -\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n\cdot\mathbf B(\mathbf r).\]

The symbol \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n\) is a vector of three spin operators, not an ordinary spatial vector. Introduce the neutron angular momentum operator

\[\widehat{\mathbf S}_n =\frac{\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol\sigma^{(S)},\]

where \(\boldsymbol\sigma^{(S)}\) is the vector of Pauli matrices in an angular-momentum basis. The physical magnetic-moment operator is

\[\boxed{ \widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n =\gamma_n\widehat{\mathbf S}_n =\mu_n\boldsymbol\sigma^{(S)},} \qquad \mu_n=\frac{\gamma_n\hbar}{2}<0.\]

The negative sign expresses that the neutron magnetic moment is antiparallel to its spin angular momentum. The dot product in the interaction means

\[\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n\cdot\mathbf B =\widehat\mu_{n,x}B_x +\widehat\mu_{n,y}B_y +\widehat\mu_{n,z}B_z.\]

Each \(\widehat\mu_{n,j}\) is a \(2\times2\) matrix, while each \(B_j(\mathbf r)\) is a real-valued classical field component. A magnetic field not aligned with the selected spin basis produces off-diagonal matrix elements and can therefore flip the neutron channel state.

The coherent nuclear interaction is represented by the Fermi pseudopotential,

\[U_{\mathrm N}(\mathbf r) =\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m_n}N(\mathbf r),\]

where \(N(\mathbf r)\) is the coherent nuclear scattering-length density. It has units of inverse area. For discrete nuclei at positions \(\mathbf R_j\), the idealized field is

\[N(\mathbf r)=\sum_j b_j\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_j),\]

where \(b_j\) is the coherent nuclear scattering length. A continuum description replaces this delta-function field by the corresponding local or coarse-grained scattering-length density.

This is already an effective low-energy description: the short-ranged strong interaction with the nuclei has been replaced by its coherent scattering length.

The scalar nuclear potential multiplies \(\mathbb I_2\) and cannot change the neutron channel in this model. The magnetic term couples the physical moment operator to the magnetic induction \(\mathbf B\) generated by the sample. Its connection to \(\mathbf M\) and its explicit matrix form are derived below.

2. Lippmann–Schwinger equation#

Using the Hamiltonian defined above, separate the free propagation from the sample interaction as

\[\widehat H_0=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_n}\nabla^2\mathbb I_2, \qquad \widehat H=\widehat H_0+\widehat U.\]

For a free neutron, \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m_n)\). The symbol \(k\) is the magnitude of the elastic incident and final wavevectors.

The stationary Schrödinger equation can then be rewritten exactly as the outgoing Lippmann–Schwinger equation

\[|\Psi^{(+)}\rangle =|\Phi\rangle +\widehat G_0^{(+)}(E)\widehat U|\Psi^{(+)}\rangle, \qquad \widehat G_0^{(+)}(E) =\left(E-\widehat H_0+i0^+\right)^{-1}.\]

Here \(|\Psi^{(+)}\rangle\) denotes the complete quantum state in abstract bra–ket notation; \(\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r)\) below is the same state represented as a two-component position-space wavefunction. \(\widehat G_0^{(+)}(E)\) is the free-particle Green operator, or resolvent.

The infinitesimal positive imaginary term \(i0^+\) specifies the outgoing boundary condition: disturbances created by the sample propagate away from it rather than toward it.

For an incoming plane wave with wavevector \(\mathbf k_i\) and normalized spinor \(\chi_i\),

\[\Phi(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r}\chi_i.\]

The outgoing free Green function in three dimensions gives

\[\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r) =\Phi(\mathbf r) -\frac{m_n}{2\pi\hbar^2} \int \frac{e^{ik|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}} {|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|} \widehat U(\mathbf r')\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r')\,d^3r'.\]

This integral equation is still exact within the effective Hamiltonian above. In particular, no Born approximation has yet been made.

3. Far field and scattering amplitude#

Far from a localized sample, \(r\gg|\mathbf r'|\), one has

\[|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'| =r-\widehat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r' +O(|\mathbf r'|^2/r),\]

where \(r=|\mathbf r|\) is the detector distance, \(\widehat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r/r\) is the observation direction, and \(\mathbf r'\) is a point inside the sample. The notation \(O(|\mathbf r'|^2/r)\) collects the next-order corrections in the ratio of sample size to detector distance. Therefore,

\[\frac{e^{ik|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}} {|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|} \sim \frac{e^{ikr}}{r}e^{-i\mathbf k_f\cdot\mathbf r'}, \qquad \mathbf k_f=k\widehat{\mathbf r}.\]

The asymptotic wavefunction is the sum of the incident plane wave and an outgoing spherical wave,

\[\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r) \sim e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r}\chi_i +\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \widehat f(\mathbf k_f,\mathbf k_i)\chi_i.\]

The exact scattering amplitude is determined by

\[\widehat f(\mathbf k_f,\mathbf k_i)\chi_i =-\frac{m_n}{2\pi\hbar^2} \int e^{-i\mathbf k_f\cdot\mathbf r} \widehat U(\mathbf r)\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.\]

It is a matrix in spin space: the sample can scatter the spatial wave and can also change the neutron spin state. The scattering amplitude has units of length. Comparing the outgoing and incident probability currents gives, for normalized initial and final spinors and elastic scattering,

\[\frac{d\sigma_{i\rightarrow f}}{d\Omega} =\left| \chi_f^{\dagger} \widehat f(\mathbf k_f,\mathbf k_i) \chi_i \right|^2.\]

Thus the spin-resolved cross section is obtained by projecting the amplitude onto the selected final spinor before taking its absolute square.

4. First Born approximation#

The first Born approximation replaces the exact wavefunction inside the scattering integral by the incoming plane wave,

\[\Psi^{(+)}(\mathbf r) \simeq e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r}\chi_i.\]

With the Fourier-space argument

\[\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i,\]

the amplitude becomes

\[\boxed{ \widehat f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol\kappa) =-\frac{m_n}{2\pi\hbar^2} \int e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r} \widehat U(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.}\]

This is the central result of the spatial scattering problem: in first order, the scattering amplitude is proportional to the Fourier transform of the interaction potential.

Important

Momentum-transfer sign

Some neutron-scattering literature defines the momentum transferred to the sample as \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k_i-\mathbf k_f\), whereas the Born integral above naturally contains \(\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i\). Thus \(\boldsymbol\kappa=-\mathbf q\). Reversing this convention replaces every Fourier amplitude by its value at \(-\mathbf q\). For real real-space fields this is its complex conjugate. The convention must be changed consistently, especially for the chiral term, which is odd under \(\mathbf q\mapsto-\mathbf q\).

The first Born approximation is a single-scattering approximation. Its validity is controlled by the size of higher terms in the Born series for the complete sample and cannot in general be reduced to a bound on one local value of the potential.

5. Why only transverse magnetization scatters#

The magnetic projection follows from Maxwell’s equations rather than from an additional assumption about the neutron. It can be derived without imposing a pointwise relation among \(\mathbf B\), \(\mathbf H\), and \(\mathbf M\). In magnetostatics, the fundamental equations for the magnetic induction are

\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J_{\mathrm{tot}},\]

where \(\mathbf J_{\mathrm{tot}}\) is the total electric-current density. A magnetization \(\mathbf M\), understood as a magnetic-dipole-moment density, represents the bound magnetization-current density

\[\mathbf J_{\mathrm M}=\nabla\times\mathbf M.\]

If \(\mathbf M\) is extended by zero outside a finite sample, this equation is understood distributionally: a discontinuity at the sample boundary also contains the corresponding surface-current contribution. The total current can therefore be separated as

\[\mathbf J_{\mathrm{tot}} =\mathbf J_{\mathrm f}+\nabla\times\mathbf M.\]

NuMagSANS considers the induction generated by the sample magnetization and assumes that no additional free electric current flows through the sample, so \(\mathbf J_{\mathrm f}=0\). The relevant real-space Maxwell equations are then

\[\boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B =\mu_0\nabla\times\mathbf M.}\]

Taking the curl of the second equation and using the vector-Laplacian identity

\[\boxed{ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf F) =\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)-\nabla^2\mathbf F}\]

gives a purely differential relation between the induction and the magnetization:

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf B =\mu_0\left[ \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf M)-\nabla^2\mathbf M \right].\]

Equivalently,

\[\boxed{ \nabla^2\mathbf B =\mu_0\left[ \nabla^2\mathbf M-\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf M) \right] =-\mu_0\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf M).}\]

Unlike a pointwise constitutive equation, this differential equation leaves harmonic field components to be fixed by boundary conditions. In particular, it does not constrain the spatially constant mode.

For the Fourier convention \(\widetilde f(\mathbf q)\propto\int f(\mathbf r) e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r\), a spatial derivative transforms as \(\nabla\rightarrow i\mathbf q\), provided boundary terms vanish or periodic boundary conditions are used. Hence, for \(\mathbf q\ne0\),

\[\mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf B}=0, \qquad \boxed{ \mathbf q\times\widetilde{\mathbf B} =\mu_0\mathbf q\times\widetilde{\mathbf M}.}\]

To solve for \(\widetilde{\mathbf B}\), take the cross product of the second equation with \(\mathbf q\) from the left. The vector triple-product identity

\[\boxed{ \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) =\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c) -\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)}\]

is often remembered as the BAC--CAB rule. Applying it to both sides gives

\[\mathbf q\times (\mathbf q\times\widetilde{\mathbf B}) =\mathbf q(\mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf B}) -q^2\widetilde{\mathbf B} =-q^2\widetilde{\mathbf B},\]

where \(\mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf B}=0\) was used. The right-hand side is

\[\mu_0\mathbf q\times (\mathbf q\times\widetilde{\mathbf M}) =\mu_0\left[ \mathbf q(\mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}) -q^2\widetilde{\mathbf M} \right].\]

Dividing by \(-q^2\) yields

\[\widetilde{\mathbf B}(\mathbf q) =\mu_0\left(\mathbb I_3 -\widehat{\mathbf q}\widehat{\mathbf q}^{\mathsf T}\right) \widetilde{\mathbf M}(\mathbf q).\]

The matrix \(\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}=\mathbb I_3-\widehat{\mathbf q} \widehat{\mathbf q}^{\mathsf T}\) is a projector: it removes the component of \(\widetilde{\mathbf M}\) parallel to \(\mathbf q\) and leaves the two perpendicular components unchanged. Indeed,

\[\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}^2=\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}, \qquad \boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widehat{\mathbf q}=0.\]

The complementary longitudinal projector is

\[\boldsymbol\Pi_{\parallel} =\widehat{\mathbf q}\widehat{\mathbf q}^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}+\boldsymbol\Pi_{\parallel}=\mathbb I_3.\]

Writing

\[\widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\parallel} =\widehat{\mathbf q} (\widehat{\mathbf q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}), \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\perp} =\widetilde{\mathbf M}-\widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\parallel},\]

the field equation simply states \(\widetilde{\mathbf B}=\mu_0\widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\perp}\). Equivalently, the triple-product identity gives the double-cross-product form

\[\widehat{\mathbf q}\times (\widehat{\mathbf q}\times\widetilde{\mathbf M}) =\widehat{\mathbf q} (\widehat{\mathbf q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}) -\widetilde{\mathbf M}.\]

NuMagSANS uses the Halpern–Johnson vector

\[\boxed{ \widetilde{\mathbf Q}(\mathbf q) =\widehat{\mathbf q} \left(\widehat{\mathbf q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}\right) -\widetilde{\mathbf M} =-\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M}.}\]

Consequently,

\[\widetilde{\mathbf B}=-\mu_0\widetilde{\mathbf Q}, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf Q}=0.\]

The longitudinal component of \(\widetilde{\mathbf M}\) therefore cannot contribute to the magnetic scattering amplitude.

Role of \(\mathbf H\) and the scattering null space#

The auxiliary field \(\mathbf H\) is not needed to derive the transverse magnetic scattering amplitude. Its useful role here is instead to identify the part of \(\mathbf M\) that lies in the null space of the scattering map. Introduce \(\mathbf H\) through its differential source equations,

\[\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_{\mathrm f}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf H=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf M.\]

Together with the differential equations for \(\mathbf B\), these imply

\[\boxed{ \nabla^2\mathbf B =\mu_0\nabla^2(\mathbf H+\mathbf M).}\]

Indeed, the difference \(\mathbf B-\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)\) has both zero divergence and zero curl and is therefore harmonic. The differential equations do not require this harmonic contribution to vanish; doing so would be an additional choice of boundary condition or of the homogeneous mode.

For \(\mathbf J_{\mathrm f}=0\) and \(\mathbf q\ne0\), the equations for \(\mathbf H\) become

\[\mathbf q\times\widetilde{\mathbf H}=0, \qquad \mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf H} =-\mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}.\]

The first equation makes \(\widetilde{\mathbf H}\) longitudinal; the second fixes its magnitude. Hence

\[\boxed{ \widetilde{\mathbf H} =-\boldsymbol\Pi_{\parallel}\widetilde{\mathbf M}, \qquad \frac{\widetilde{\mathbf B}}{\mu_0} =\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M}.}\]

This is the longitudinal–transverse split relevant to magnetic SANS. The transverse part is represented by the SANS-active induction, whereas the longitudinal part satisfies \(\boldsymbol\Pi_{\parallel}\widetilde{\mathbf M} =-\widetilde{\mathbf H}\) and lies in the null space of the magnetic scattering operator. The minus sign follows from \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf H=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf M\).

Gauge freedom of the magnetization#

The transverse projection also exposes a non-uniqueness of the magnetization that is relevant to magnetic scattering. Let \(\chi(\mathbf r)\) be a sufficiently regular scalar field, with boundary conditions compatible with the Fourier transform. The transformation

\[\boxed{ \mathbf M(\mathbf r) \longrightarrow \mathbf M'(\mathbf r) =\mathbf M(\mathbf r)+\nabla\chi(\mathbf r)}\]

does not change the bound magnetization-current density, because

\[\nabla\times\mathbf M' =\nabla\times\mathbf M +\underbrace{\nabla\times\nabla\chi}_{=0} =\nabla\times\mathbf M.\]

At fixed boundary conditions it therefore leaves the magnetostatic Maxwell equations above, and hence every finite-\(q\) component of \(\mathbf B\), unchanged. The auxiliary longitudinal field transforms in the opposite direction,

\[\mathbf H' = \mathbf H-\nabla\chi,\]

so that its differential source equations remain satisfied. The invariance of the induction is especially transparent in Fourier space:

\[\widetilde{\mathbf M}' =\widetilde{\mathbf M} +i\mathbf q\widetilde\chi,\]
\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M}' &=\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M} +i\widetilde\chi\, \underbrace{\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\mathbf q}_{=0} \\ &=\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Consequently,

\[\widetilde{\mathbf B}[\mathbf M'] =\widetilde{\mathbf B}[\mathbf M], \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf Q}[\mathbf M'] =\widetilde{\mathbf Q}[\mathbf M].\]

This is the magnetization gauge freedom meant here: at every \(\mathbf q\ne0\), magnetic SANS distinguishes only the equivalence class

\[\mathbf M\sim\mathbf M+\nabla\chi,\]

not the longitudinal part of \(\mathbf M\) separately. This statement does not mean that the microscopic magnetization is physically arbitrary. It means that the scattering map from \(\mathbf M\) to \(\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\) is not injective: magnetization fields that differ only by such a longitudinal contribution produce the same magnetic SANS signal.

The relation can also be expressed through the Helmholtz decomposition

\[\mathbf M=\mathbf M_{\perp}+\nabla\phi, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf M_{\perp}=0.\]

Taking the divergence gives the Poisson equation

\[\nabla^2\phi=\nabla\cdot\mathbf M,\]

whose solution depends on the chosen boundary conditions. Subtracting \(\nabla\phi\) is a possible gauge choice; it selects the transverse representative. In Fourier space this representative is uniquely given by

\[\widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\perp} =\boldsymbol\Pi_{\perp}\widetilde{\mathbf M}, \qquad \mathbf q\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}_{\perp}=0.\]

This Helmholtz decomposition is the real-space counterpart of the double-cross-product projector derived above.

All projector and gauge statements in this section apply to \(\mathbf q\ne0\). A magnetization \(\mathbf M_0\) that is constant over all space belongs entirely to the \(\mathbf q=0\) mode. The differential Maxwell equations do not then enforce \(\mathbf H_0=-\mathbf M_0\); the homogeneous components of \(\mathbf B\) and \(\mathbf H\) must be fixed by boundary conditions. By contrast, a magnetization that is constant only inside a finite sample, \(\mathbf M(\mathbf r)=\mathbf M_0\mathbb 1_V(\mathbf r)\), is not a pure zero mode. Its boundary and shape function generate finite-\(q\) components that can contribute to SANS.

A spatially uniform external guide field likewise belongs to the separate \(\mathbf q=0\) mode. It sets the spin quantization axis but is not part of the finite-\(q\) SANS signal. If free currents are present, their Fourier-space contribution must be added and the magnetization-only projector is no longer the complete field solution.

The magnetic scattering-length coefficient is

\[b_{\mathrm H} =\frac{m_n|\mu_n|\mu_0}{2\pi\hbar^2} \simeq2.91\times10^8\ \mathrm{A^{-1}m^{-1}},\]

when \(\mathbf M\) is expressed in \(\mathrm{A/m}\).

6. The NuMagSANS scattering operator#

From this point onward, \(\mathbf q\) denotes the Fourier-space argument used by NuMagSANS. If momentum transfer is defined with the opposite sign in an experimental convention, the conversion described in the note above must be applied.

NuMagSANS uses the unitary Fourier transforms

\[\widetilde N(\mathbf q) =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}} \int N(\mathbf r)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r,\]
\[\widetilde{\mathbf M}(\mathbf q) =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}} \int \mathbf M(\mathbf r)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r.\]

Since \(N\) has units \(\mathrm{m^{-2}}\), its transform \(\widetilde N\) has units of length. Likewise, \(\widetilde{\mathbf M}\) has units \(\mathrm{A\,m^2}\), so \(b_{\mathrm H}\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\) also has units of length. The nuclear and magnetic terms can therefore be added at the amplitude level.

The physical moment was written above in an angular-momentum basis as \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n =\mu_n\boldsymbol\sigma^{(S)}\) with \(\mu_n<0\). For the NuMagSANS channel labels it is convenient to introduce

\[\boldsymbol\sigma=-\boldsymbol\sigma^{(S)}.\]

The same physical operator is then

\[\widehat{\boldsymbol\mu}_n=|\mu_n|\boldsymbol\sigma.\]

Thus a \(+\) eigenstate of the channel matrix \(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\widehat{\mathbf P}\) has its magnetic moment along \(\widehat{\mathbf P}\) and its angular momentum in the opposite direction. This relabeling changes no physics, but it must be retained when assigning \(+\) and \(-\) cross sections. Using \(\widetilde{\mathbf B}=-\mu_0\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\) in the Zeeman interaction gives

\[\widetilde{\widehat U}(\mathbf q) =\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m_n} \left[ \widetilde N(\mathbf q)\mathbb I_2 +b_{\mathrm H}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot \widetilde{\mathbf Q}(\mathbf q) \right].\]

The first-Born amplitude therefore equals a common factor \(-(2\pi)^{3/2}\) times the following coherent scattering-length operator:

\[\boxed{ \widehat{\mathcal A}(\mathbf q) =\widetilde N(\mathbf q)\mathbb I_2 +b_{\mathrm H}\, \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf Q}(\mathbf q).}\]

Here \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) denotes the Pauli matrices in the channel basis used below. This one operator contains the nuclear amplitude, the magnetic amplitude, and their relative phase.

Important

Sign and channel convention

The neutron gyromagnetic ratio is negative. Its sign can instead be carried explicitly in the magnetic-moment operator, but the labels of the two channel states must then be reversed. NuMagSANS fixes the convention operationally by the boxed equation above together with \(\widetilde{\mathbf Q}=\widehat{\mathbf q} (\widehat{\mathbf q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf M}) -\widetilde{\mathbf M}\) and by defining the first superscript of \(\Sigma^{s_i s_f}\) as the incident state. Once these definitions are chosen, the sign of the nuclear–magnetic interference and the assignment of the two chiral spin-flip channels are fixed; they are not individually arbitrary.

For a scattering volume \(V\), the matrix element between normalized initial and final channel spinors is converted into the macroscopic differential cross section according to

\[\boxed{ \frac{d\Sigma^{s_i s_f}}{d\Omega} =\frac{8\pi^3}{V} \left| \chi_{s_f}^{\dagger} \widehat{\mathcal A}(\mathbf q) \chi_{s_i} \right|^2.}\]

The factor \(8\pi^3\) is not an additional physical interaction. It is the square of \((2\pi)^{3/2}\), which appears when the unnormalized Born integral is expressed through the unitary Fourier transforms above.

7. Spin projection#

Let \(\widehat{\mathbf P}\) be the real unit vector defining the neutron quantization axis. Complete it to a right-handed orthonormal basis \((\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\widehat{\mathbf P})\). In this basis the three channel Pauli matrices are

\[\begin{split}\sigma_1= \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_2= \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_{\parallel}= \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

The two normalized channel spinors are explicitly

\[\begin{split}\chi_+=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \chi_-=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}\]

and satisfy

\[(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\widehat{\mathbf P})\chi_{\pm} =\pm\chi_{\pm}.\]

The generally complex Halpern–Johnson amplitude can be resolved in the same real-space basis,

\[\widetilde{\mathbf Q} =Q_1\mathbf e_1+Q_2\mathbf e_2+Q_{\parallel}\widehat{\mathbf P}, \qquad Q_{\parallel}=\widehat{\mathbf P}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf Q}.\]

Consequently,

\[\begin{split}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf Q} =\sigma_1Q_1+\sigma_2Q_2+\sigma_{\parallel}Q_{\parallel} =\begin{pmatrix} Q_{\parallel}&Q_1-iQ_2\\ Q_1+iQ_2&-Q_{\parallel} \end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

In the \((+,-)\) basis the scattering operator becomes

\[\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal A} = \begin{pmatrix} \widetilde N+b_{\mathrm H}Q_{\parallel} & b_{\mathrm H}(Q_1-iQ_2) \\ b_{\mathrm H}(Q_1+iQ_2) & \widetilde N-b_{\mathrm H}Q_{\parallel} \end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

Rows in this matrix label the final state in the order \((+,-)\), and columns label the initial state in the same order. The lower-left element, for example, is therefore the amplitude for \(+\rightarrow-\). The diagonal entries preserve the channel state and the off-diagonal entries flip it. We use the NuMagSANS convention that the first sign in \(s_i s_f\) labels the incident neutron and the second sign labels the scattered neutron. Thus, for example, \(+-\) means \(+\rightarrow-\).

It is useful to define the following five real building blocks, with \(K=8\pi^3/V\):

\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm N}}{d\Omega} =K|\widetilde N|^2,\]
\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm M}}{d\Omega} =K b_{\mathrm H}^2 \widetilde{\mathbf Q}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf Q}^{\ast},\]
\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm P}}{d\Omega} =K b_{\mathrm H}^2 \left|\widehat{\mathbf P}\cdot \widetilde{\mathbf Q}\right|^2,\]
\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{NM}}}{d\Omega} =K b_{\mathrm H}\widehat{\mathbf P}\cdot \left( \widetilde N\widetilde{\mathbf Q}^{\ast} +\widetilde N^{\ast}\widetilde{\mathbf Q} \right),\]
\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\chi}}{d\Omega} =-iK b_{\mathrm H}^2\widehat{\mathbf P}\cdot \left( \widetilde{\mathbf Q}\times \widetilde{\mathbf Q}^{\ast} \right).\]

The cross product in the last expression is purely imaginary, so the chiral cross section is real. The Pauli identity

\[(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a) (\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b) =(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\mathbb I_2 +i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\]

then gives all four channels.

Non-spin-flip channels#

For the diagonal elements one simply expands

\[|\widetilde N\pm b_{\mathrm H}Q_{\parallel}|^2 =|\widetilde N|^2+b_{\mathrm H}^2|Q_{\parallel}|^2 \pm b_{\mathrm H} (\widetilde NQ_{\parallel}^{\ast} +\widetilde N^{\ast}Q_{\parallel}).\]

The three terms are the nuclear, longitudinal magnetic, and interference contributions. Hence,

\[\boxed{ \frac{d\Sigma^{++}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm N}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{NM}}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm P}}{d\Omega},}\]
\[\boxed{ \frac{d\Sigma^{--}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm N}}{d\Omega} -\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{NM}}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm P}}{d\Omega}.}\]

The nuclear–magnetic term changes sign because reversing the incident and analyzed channel changes the sign of the longitudinal magnetic amplitude but not the nuclear amplitude.

Spin-flip channels#

Only the part of \(\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\) perpendicular to \(\widehat{\mathbf P}\) can connect the two channel states. Define

\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{sf}}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm M}}{d\Omega} -\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm P}}{d\Omega}.\]

The difference above equals \(Kb_{\mathrm H}^2(|Q_1|^2+|Q_2|^2)\). The imbalance between the two off-diagonal matrix elements follows from

\[|Q_1\pm iQ_2|^2 =|Q_1|^2+|Q_2|^2 \mp i(Q_1Q_2^{\ast}-Q_2Q_1^{\ast}),\]

with

\[\widehat{\mathbf P}\cdot (\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\times\widetilde{\mathbf Q}^{\ast}) =Q_1Q_2^{\ast}-Q_2Q_1^{\ast}.\]

The off-diagonal matrix elements therefore give

\[\boxed{ \frac{d\Sigma^{+-}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{sf}}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma_{\chi}}{d\Omega},}\]
\[\boxed{ \frac{d\Sigma^{-+}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{sf}}}{d\Omega} -\frac{d\Sigma_{\chi}}{d\Omega}.}\]

Consequently, the polarization-independent spin-flip and chiral terms are the half-sum and half-difference of the measurable spin-flip channels,

\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm{sf}}}{d\Omega} =\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{d\Sigma^{+-}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma^{-+}}{d\Omega} \right),\]
\[\frac{d\Sigma_{\chi}}{d\Omega} =\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{d\Sigma^{+-}}{d\Omega} -\frac{d\Sigma^{-+}}{d\Omega} \right).\]

Unpolarized and half-polarized measurements#

Averaging over the two equally populated incident states and summing over the unobserved final state removes the nuclear–magnetic and chiral terms. The result is the sum of the nuclear and unpolarized magnetic contributions,

\[\frac{1}{2} \sum_{s_i,s_f} \frac{d\Sigma^{s_i s_f}}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm N}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma_{\mathrm M}}{d\Omega}.\]

If the incident beam is polarized but the outgoing spin is not analyzed, the SANSPOL cross sections are

\[\frac{d\Sigma^+}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma^{++}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma^{+-}}{d\Omega}, \qquad \frac{d\Sigma^-}{d\Omega} =\frac{d\Sigma^{--}}{d\Omega} +\frac{d\Sigma^{-+}}{d\Omega}.\]

These are precisely the combinations exported by the corresponding NuMagSANS output options.

8. NuMagSANS SANS geometry#

NuMagSANS fixes the incident beam along the \(x\)-axis. In the small-angle approximation the detector lies in the \(yz\)-plane and

\[\begin{split}\mathbf q =q\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf q} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

Inserting this direction into the transverse projector gives the components actually evaluated in the kernels,

\[\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathbf Q} =\begin{pmatrix} -\widetilde M_x \\ (\widetilde M_z\sin\theta -\widetilde M_y\cos\theta)\cos\theta \\ (\widetilde M_y\cos\theta -\widetilde M_z\sin\theta)\sin\theta \end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

The general spin-projection formulas above remain valid for any real unit polarization vector \(\widehat{\mathbf P}\). Choosing a particular guide field direction simply specifies which component of \(\widetilde{\mathbf Q}\) is longitudinal in spin space.

The complete list of output names, formulas, symmetries, and configuration examples is given under Output Options in the Parameter Overview.

9. Equivalent derivation from Fermi’s golden rule#

For completeness, take plane waves normalized in a box of volume \(\mathcal V\),

\[\langle\mathbf r|\mathbf k,s\rangle =\frac{e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}}{\sqrt{\mathcal V}}\chi_s.\]

To first order, Fermi’s golden rule gives

\[w_{i\rightarrow f} =\frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |\langle f|\widehat U|i\rangle|^2 \delta(E_f-E_i).\]

Using the final-state density \(\mathcal Vd^3k_f/(2\pi)^3\), integrating the energy delta function, and dividing by the incident flux \(j_i=\hbar k_i/(m_n\mathcal V)\) yields

\[\frac{d\sigma_{i\rightarrow f}}{d\Omega} =\frac{k_f}{k_i} \left(\frac{m_n}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^2 \left| \int e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r} \chi_f^{\dagger}\widehat U(\mathbf r)\chi_i\,d^3r \right|^2.\]

For elastic scattering \(k_f/k_i=1\), and this is exactly the modulus squared of the first-Born amplitude obtained from the Lippmann–Schwinger equation. The two routes therefore contain the same first-order physics: one extracts an outgoing wave, while the other computes its transition rate.

10. What changes beyond this model?#

The compact NuMagSANS formulas are a consequence of the assumptions stated at the beginning. Relaxing them changes the problem as follows:

  • Inelastic scattering: the cross section depends on energy transfer and time-dependent correlation functions, and contains the factor \(k_f/k_i\).

  • Spin-dependent nuclear scattering: the nuclear term is no longer a scalar multiple of \(\mathbb I_2\); nuclear spin-incoherent and nuclear–magnetic terms must be added.

  • Multiple scattering or strong refraction: the incident plane wave cannot replace the exact wave inside the sample; the first Born approximation is insufficient.

  • Statistical ensembles: amplitudes are added coherently within one realization, whereas the physically appropriate ensemble average is normally taken over intensities or correlation functions afterward.

  • Instrumental effects: finite polarization, analyzer efficiency, wavelength spread, detector resolution, and background act on the ideal cross sections after the sample-scattering calculation.

Successful execution of NuMagSANS verifies the deterministic calculation of the selected model. Scientific validation still requires suitable analytical limits, symmetry relations, convergence studies, and comparison with reference data or experiment.
